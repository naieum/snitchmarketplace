# Camera and optics decisions

Read when framing, viewpoint, field of view, focus, or movement is central to the request.
Start with the information the shot must convey, the subject's action, and the viewer's
orientation. The emotional effect of a choice depends on the sequence around it.

## Distinguish the controls

- **Shot size** is how much of the subject appears. It is a framing outcome, not a focal
  length. A wide frame may show a room; a close frame may isolate a hand or face.
- **Viewpoint** is the camera's position and orientation in the world. It determines
  perspective and what can be seen or concealed. Name it using the set geography.
- **Field of view** controls how much the lens/crop includes at that viewpoint. A longer
  focal length on the same format narrows it. Changing focal length at a fixed viewpoint
  changes framing without changing the relative perspective of objects.
- **Subject distance** changes perspective. To preserve subject size with a wider view,
  moving closer exaggerates near/far size relationships; a longer view from farther back
  makes them look closer together. Check whether the set has the needed distance.
- **Focus** selects which depth reads clearly. Depth of field varies with format, focal
  length, aperture, focus distance, and viewing conditions; a shallow look is not a lens
  number by itself. A focus pull changes the sharp plane, not camera position.

When specifying millimeters, state the camera format/gate or a full-frame-equivalent
assumption. A number copied across a phone, large sensor, virtual camera, and drawn scene
is not a consistent image instruction. If the medium is flat 2D, use crop, layer scale,
and perspective intent instead of fake glass specifications. For 3D, camera and depth
settings need an actual renderer/layout check. Never promise precise depth, blur, or exposure
from prose alone.

## Build a shot

Use `purpose → placement → frame → action → transition`. Example: “The guest realizes the
cup is chipped; place a south-side medium two-shot so both hands and faces are visible;
let the guest turn the rim away; hold for the host's noticing glance; cut to the established
rim detail only when the guest reaches for it.” That instruction gives an action and a
reason to change shots. “Cinematic 50mm push” does not.

| Choice | Common use | Failure to check |
|---|---|---|
| Wide/full | Establish geography or let a physical action play continuously | The clue is too small at the viewing size. |
| Medium/two-shot | Hold exchange and listening together | Foreground overlap hides the handoff. |
| Close/detail | Isolate a decision, reaction, or clue | The audience loses whose hand or object this is. |
| Subjective view | Restrict knowledge to one observer | It sees something the observer could not see. |
| High/low view | Reveal a relationship at another elevation | “Power” is asserted without support from the scene. |
| Deep focus | Let background action coexist with foreground | Two competing attention targets obscure the intended reveal. |
| Focus shift | Move attention between depth planes | The start, trigger, and destination are unspecified. |

## Motion has a path

A pan or tilt rotates the camera; a dolly/truck/crane translates it; an orbit travels
around a subject; a zoom changes field of view at a fixed position. Handheld describes a
movement character, not a reason to move. A dolly zoom combines translation with an
opposing field-of-view change; state the framing goal if requested. Subject motion is
separate. “Locked off” can coexist with a performer crossing frame, but not with a camera
orbit in the same interval.

For each move, write the start framing, trigger, world-space direction/path, pace,
foreground/background behavior when relevant, and end framing. Use camera-left, screen-left,
and character-left precisely. If a move crosses the scene's action axis, say how the audience
reorients. A still shot may be the strongest choice when it lets a decision or visual change
occur inside a stable comparison.

Record aspect ratio and intended viewing surface. A composition that reads in a theater may
hide a small clue in a phone crop. If multiple ratios are required, identify a protected
image area or create alternative frames; do not assume one centered crop preserves eyelines,
props, and action.
