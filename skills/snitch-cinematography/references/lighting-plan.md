# Lighting plan

Read when light, contrast, color, or matching across angles matters. Design for the scene's
information first, then for mood. The plan must say where light comes from in the story
world and which surfaces need to read. An attractive still that hides a required gesture
fails the scene.

## Source map

For each scene, record: source ID and type (sun, window, visible practical, motivated
fixture, stylized source), world position/direction, quality (hard/soft, broad/narrow),
color relationship, intensity relationship, shadow or falloff intention, and what it
illuminates. Use relative terms unless location and camera tests support numbers. A
practical lamp may motivate illumination supplied by additional fixtures; distinguish the
visible story source from actual lighting gear.

One simple map can prevent contradictory reverse angles:

```text
North: window W1, cool soft daylight
Center: table T1, action between east and west chairs
East: warm practical lamp P1, visible in master
South: planned camera side for this exchange
```

The world positions stay fixed when the camera reverses. Screen-left changes with camera
position; the window does not jump from north to south. If time passes or a lamp switches,
record the cue and the new state. Match the light's direction on faces, hands, walls, and
important props. An intentional relight for clarity can be proposed, but label the cheat
and its continuity cost.

## Decide what is visible

- **Key-to-shadow relationship:** which side holds the main shape, and whether the viewer
  must still read eyes, hands, a clue, or facial expression.
- **Background hierarchy:** what is legible and what recedes. A dramatic low-key look can
  retain a precise prop highlight; a bright look can still isolate one subject.
- **Color:** distinguish light color from surface color and final grade. A blue wall under
  amber light is not the same design fact as a neutral wall with a blue grade.
- **Motivation:** moving clouds, swinging practicals, vehicle lights, screens, and fire can
  change the pattern during a shot; describe the change and continuity needs.
- **Medium:** in 2D, use value grouping, shadow shapes, and layer/color rules; in 3D, note
  key/fill/rim/ambient intent without guessing renderer values; in live action, note fixture
  placement/power/access as tests.

When the user gives a no-new-equipment limit, make use of the existing source or propose
blocking/exposure changes consistent with it. Do not silently introduce a large rig. An
unmeasured room does not justify a precise stop, lux figure, light distance, or power draw.
A render or camera test is needed before claiming those are achieved.

## Shot light card

For an essential shot, record `source(s) | world direction | subject and background
readability | contrast/color intent | change during action | matching shots | test need`.
Carry any design-owned materials or paint colors as supplied facts; if a wardrobe or wall
change is required to make the image work, propose it to production design rather than
silently changing the asset bible. State alternative solutions when conflicting needs
exist, with the story consequence of each.
