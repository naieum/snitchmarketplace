# Design a scene that can be staged

Read when designing a location or auditing whether an existing layout supports the action.
Establish world directions and landmarks before screen directions or camera angles.

## From script to place

For each scene, extract required entrances/exits, actions, object transfers, concealments,
reveals, travel distances, and changes in who can see or reach what. Then place doors,
windows, furniture, practical sources, hiding places, and hero props accordingly. A
layout must make the scripted behavior possible. If it cannot, propose a specific design
or blocking change and identify who owns the decision.

Use an overhead sketch in words when needed:

```text
North: window W1; sofa S1 beneath it
West: entry door D1, swings inward northward
Center: table T1; hero box PR04 starts on its east edge
East: exit D2; character path from D1 to T1 to D2 remains clear
South: open wall or camera-access zone, subject to location confirmation
```

This is a design proposal unless measured or sourced. Label supplied dimensions as facts
and assumed dimensions as estimates. A drawn location may have controllable walls; a
found room may not. Do not invent a removable wall, clearance, permit, or build budget.

## Visibility and hidden information

Map who can see what from their position. A reflection, open doorway, window, or cutaway
wall can reveal a supposedly hidden object. Plan concealment in world space first, then
let camera or storyboard plans select viewpoints. If a reveal depends on a prop turning,
record its facing and the action that changes it. If the audience sees something a
character cannot, make that distinction explicit.

Name action surfaces with practical requirements: stable tabletop, space for a fight or
dance, a door that can be opened on cue, a ledge reachable by the character, or an object
that can be reset. Offer a usable lower-cost layout alternative if a supplied cast/location
limit is tight, while preserving the story action.

## Production method

Live action needs access for performers, camera and crew, safe paths, sound-sensitive
surfaces, power if required, and reset positions. Exact dimensions and safety claims need
a location survey and appropriate department review. Stop motion needs animator access
and repeatable marks. 2D needs artwork for revealed space and consistent layer order. 3D
needs geometry beyond selected framing if the camera moves. A generated shot needs a
self-contained world description and explicit state at each shot boundary; text alone
cannot confirm the output obeys it.

The design handoff states named landmarks, orientation, entrances, action paths, source
positions, asset IDs, initial and final states, assumed dimensions, unavailable views,
and what camera or layout should verify. Do not choose shot sizes in this artifact; give
the space in which a shot decision can be made.
