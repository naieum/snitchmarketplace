# Visual system for the film world

Read for a multi-scene visual bible or a scene whose style is unsettled. The visual system
makes repeated design decisions predictable. It does not replace scene-specific assets or
playable geography.

## Start from story and medium

State the time/place and what the audience should recognize immediately. Inventory repeated
ideas: orderly versus improvised spaces, public versus private faces, new versus worn
materials, accessible versus forbidding architecture. Choose a few film-wide rules with a
story reason: silhouette shapes, scale relationships, material finish, palette roles,
pattern density, wear, and the treatment of signage or text. Avoid assigning a stock
emotional meaning to a color or shape without testing it in this story.

Example: “Public rooms have repeated straight partitions and little personal clutter;
private rooms expose hand-repaired surfaces. When a character brings a repaired object
into a public room, its irregular edge remains visible.” This describes a repeatable visual
contrast and a prop payoff. “Dark cinematic world” does not.

## Palette and material records

Record what color belongs to a surface or costume, what changes under different light,
and what is a finishing intent. Use descriptive ranges until exact colors are supplied or
approved. A palette may assign background, character, focal prop, and accent roles; it
need not dictate identical colors in every shot. Check value contrast as well as hue so
key information reads in grayscale, small format, and lighting changes.

Materials carry practical information: shiny glass can reveal an off-screen character or
crew; a matte surface may hold a projected shadow; a sheer curtain changes privacy and
light. Define whether texture is drawn, built, modeled, photographed, or composited.
Distinguish reference for silhouette, palette, surface, and mood. If a reference is a
user-supplied work, name the trait you are using; do not reproduce an entire protected
character or exact composition as an unnamed default.

## World logic and variation

Document physical or stylized rules that affect story action: whether doors slide or swing,
what scale characters have relative to furniture, whether shapes bend, how wet materials
change, or which signs the audience can read. Rules that only decorate a prompt are less
useful than rules that prevent a broken scene. Allow deliberate exceptions and record when
they happen. Keep variation bounded for a returning location: time of day, damage, seasonal
decor, and occupancy can change while its doors, windows, and basic orientation persist.

## Medium-specific choices

- Live action: distinguish found-location facts from set dressing proposals, construction,
  costume, and finish. Flag what location access and measurements must confirm.
- 2D animation: define layer order, reusable backgrounds, silhouette constraints, and the
  viewpoints that require new drawings. Camera language may mean artwork movement.
- 3D animation: define scale, topology/rig implications, texture/material relationships,
  and off-camera geometry the camera may reveal.
- Stop motion: define puppet/set scale, material durability, replacement parts, and access
  for frame-by-frame adjustment.
- Mixed or generated media: define invariant appearance and scene states in text plus the
  actual references available. Do not claim a reference guarantees consistency.

End with a concise visual rule list and the specific scene/asset decisions it governs.
If a story or production constraint makes one rule fail, revise the rule or record a
purposeful exception rather than silently breaking it.
