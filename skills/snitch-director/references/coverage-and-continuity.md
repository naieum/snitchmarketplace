# Coverage, continuity, edit, and sound

Read when building or auditing a sequence. Coverage means the material needed to tell this
scene; it is not a fixed requirement for one wide, two mediums, and two close-ups.

## Build the edit on paper

For each story beat, name the shot that communicates the action, the changed state, and the
necessary response. One shot may do all three. Mark essential shots and optional alternatives.
A master can establish the space and provide an edit fallback; do not add one mechanically
if another design already does that work. An insert should carry information, not just fill
a gap. A continuous take needs performance and attention changes even without cuts.

For adjacent selected shots, state why the cut occurs and what makes it readable: matched
action, eyeline, sound bridge, revelation, emotional shift, or an intended contrast. A cut
on action needs compatible stages of the same action, not two complete repetitions. A
reaction must follow or intentionally anticipate what the character can know. Check a
returning wide against the state created in the details.

The action axis connects the key participants or follows the dominant motion. Staying on
one side usually preserves their screen relationship. A deliberate crossing can work through
a visible continuous move, a re-establishing view, or an intentional disorientation. Record
which and why. Do not flag every crossing automatically, and do not claim that a random
insert repairs geography. The spatial 180-degree convention is unrelated to shutter angle.

Eyelines need plausible direction and height. Entrances/exits and movement should preserve
travel direction unless a turn or new orientation is communicated. Similar consecutive
angles can create an unwanted jump; use a meaningful change in view or a deliberate jump
cut with a stated purpose. Neither degree counts nor the presence of a wide shot alone
prove the cut works.

## Continuity ledger

Track meaningful state at shot boundaries, including alternatives intended to cover the same
action. Use `shot ID | entry state | action/change | exit state | next-shot dependency`.
Include prop holder/hand/position, costume/damage, door and light state, position/facing,
eyeline, action phase, time/weather, and character knowledge when relevant. Carry a stable
asset's invariants separately from this mutable state. Mark intentional discontinuities.

For nonchronological shooting or generation, supply entry states explicitly; do not assume
the previous batch item is the previous story shot. After a change, name affected downstream
shots, references, and audio/edit cues. Update the plan's version instead of silently replacing
what another department is using.

## Rhythm and sound

Shot length follows information and performance. Let a viewer register the setup before
removing it, and the response before racing onward. Faster cuts do not automatically create
more tension. A held frame, delayed reverse, or withheld sound may serve the scene better.

Separate dialogue, ambience, discrete effects, and music intent. Specify on-screen/off-screen
source, perspective, cue, and intended silence. A sound may start before its image (audio
lead) or continue across the next image (audio tail). State the relationship and timeline
position; these do not necessarily add screen duration. Sound can prepare or conceal a reveal,
so check it against the scene's information plan. A cue sheet is not a composed score,
recorded performance, licensed track, or finished mix.

## Verification boundaries

A written plan can establish intended matching, coverage, and timing arithmetic. Inspect
actual frames/clips/audio to judge whether execution matches it. Note exactly which assets
and intervals were inspected. With a still, movement and timing remain unverified; with
silent playback, sound remains unverified. If tools cannot access supplied media, work from
available descriptions with explicit limits, not invented observations.
