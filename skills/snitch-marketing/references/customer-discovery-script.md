# Customer Discovery Script

The single highest-leverage activity for any indie SaaS, content brand, or solo consultant in the next 60 days is getting on the phone with 10 paying customers and 10 free-tier users who haven't converted, and asking a structured set of questions about what's true and not true about why they did or didn't pay.

This reference is the script. It's surfaced from STEP 4 when the audit detects a customer-language gap (homepage hero is generic, no named testimonials, positioning unclear, ICP fuzzy). The script replaces guessing-at-language with using the words the customers themselves use.

## Why this beats every alternative

- Surveys give you vendor-framed answers (the customer answers what the form asks, not what they actually feel).
- Reading testimonials gives you only the wins (selection bias toward happy users).
- Reading reviews gives you only the angry (selection bias the other way).
- Customer-discovery calls give you the median experience plus the language they use to describe it.

Recordings of 20 calls become the input data for hero copy, FAQ wording, comparison tables, ad creative, and trust-strip claims. Skipping discovery and writing copy from intuition produces homepage prose that lands for nobody.

## When to recommend customer discovery

The audit recommends discovery when:

- Hero copy uses generic language ("for everyone who [verb]") without segment specificity.
- No named testimonials exist on the site (Cat 74 / Cat 60 finding).
- Pricing copy doesn't name competitor alternatives (Cat 81 positioning gap).
- The brand has 50+ paying customers but the team can't articulate the canonical buyer in one sentence.
- The team is debating "should we be for X or Y" without scored evidence (the site names no single buyer — Cat 81).

## The three rules (read these before the script)

The script's questions only produce signal when applied with three discipline rules. Skipping these rules turns the call into a feedback session that confirms what the founder already believed, which is worse than no call. The rules:

**Rule 1: Talk about their life, not your idea.** The customer's existing workflow, frustrations, current tools, and recent decisions are real data. Their opinion of your idea is not. As soon as the conversation drifts to "what do you think of this?" or "would you use this if it had X feature?", you've left the data zone.

**Rule 2: Ask about specifics in the past, not generics or hypotheticals about the future.** "Tell me about the last time this came up" produces signal. "How often does this come up?" produces a generic estimate that humans almost always inflate. "Would you pay for this?" produces near-universal yes that doesn't match buying behavior.

**Rule 3: Talk less, listen more.** The instinct under social pressure is to fill silence, defend the product, or steer toward the answer you want. Resist all three. A customer who is talking is producing data. A founder who is talking is producing wishful thinking.

These three rules shape every question below. Re-read them before each call.

## The script

Half-hour calls, recorded with permission, transcribed afterward. The interviewer asks; the customer talks. Don't pitch. Don't react. Don't correct.

```
1. (Warm-up) What were you doing the day you decided to look for this?
   What were you using before?

2. (Pain) Why didn't [previous solution] work?
   How often did the pain come up before you searched?

3. (Trigger) What happened on the day you actually downloaded / signed up?
   What did you type into Google? Or what did you click?

4. (First use) Walk me through the first 24 hours after install / signup.
   When did you feel like "okay, this is going to work"?
   When did you feel like "this is annoying"?

5. (Money, past behavior) What did you pay last for software like this?
   When you bought it, what made you actually click pay (not just sign up)?
   At what price would [product] feel obviously cheap? Obviously expensive?

6. (Comparison) What else did you try before / after this?
   Why didn't you go with that?

7. (Recommendation, past behavior) Who do you know who has the same problem?
   Have you already told anyone about [product]? Who specifically?
   What did you tell them?

8. (Catch) If we shut [product] down tomorrow, what would you do?

9. (Spontaneous referral) Have you mentioned [product] to anyone in the last
   30 days, unprompted? Tell me what made you bring it up.
```

The Q5 modification (asking what made them click pay, not just sign up) extracts the actual buying-trigger moment. The Q7 + Q9 split distinguishes "would you recommend it" (future hypothetical, worthless) from "have you already mentioned it" (past behavior, the gold-standard advancement signal: a customer who has spontaneously told someone else about the product is a customer who really uses it).

## How to run the calls

- **Permission**: ask "Can I record this and review the recording later?" at the top. Most customers say yes.
- **Mute yourself between questions**. Silence prompts the customer to elaborate. Don't fill it.
- **Don't pitch**. The call is to learn, not to convert.
- **Don't correct misconceptions** the customer has about the product unless they ask directly. Their misconception IS the data.
- **Don't react to negative feedback**. "Tell me more" is the only valid response. Defending the product on a discovery call wastes the call.
- **Listen for verbatim language**. The exact phrases the customer uses to describe the pain go on the homepage as hero copy.
- **Compliments are worthless**. When the customer says "this is a great idea" or "I love this", the only useful response is to ignore the compliment and probe for past behavior: "Tell me about the last time you ran into this problem. What did you do?" Compliments produce social warmth, not signal. The founder who leaves the call buoyed by compliments and no commitments has been politely dismissed.
- **End every call with a commitment or an advancement.** A commitment is the customer giving up something real (time, money, reputation) on behalf of the product: a paid signup, a referral, a public testimonial, an introduction to two more interviewees, a calendar block to test the next iteration. An advancement is the call moving the customer measurably closer to a commitment: a follow-up call scheduled, a beta-access request, an email subscription. Without a commitment or an advancement, the call was theater. Always anchor at the end with a specific ask: "Could you intro me to two people who'd benefit?", "Can I follow up next month with the next version?", or "Would you be willing to leave a testimonial if I send you the questions?"

## Question patterns that lie vs question patterns that surface truth

The single biggest reason customer-discovery calls produce confused-but-confident founders is that the questions invite social-niceness lies. The patterns below are the most common failure modes and the past-behavior reframes that produce honest answers.

| What people ask (lies) | What to ask instead (truth) |
|---|---|
| "Would you buy this?" | "What did you pay last for software like this? When you bought it, what made you click pay?" |
| "How important is this problem to you?" | "When was the last time this came up? Walk me through what you did." |
| "Do you like this idea?" | "Tell me about the last time you ran into [this problem]. What did you do?" |
| "What features would you want?" | "Tell me about your workflow today. What's the worst part of it?" |
| "Would you recommend this?" | "Who do you know with the same problem? Have you already told anyone about [tool]? What did you say?" |
| "Is the price fair?" | "What did you pay last for X? At what price would [product] feel obviously cheap? Obviously expensive?" |
| "Would you switch from your current solution?" | "Walk me through how you ended up using [current solution]. What was the trigger? What's stopping you from using something different today?" |
| "Is this a problem you have?" | "What did you do this morning / today / yesterday related to [adjacent area of life]?" |

The general pattern: replace "would you" / "do you like" / "is it important" with "tell me about the last time" or "what did you do." The first set asks for opinion or future hypothetical, both of which inflate. The second set asks for past behavior, which the customer can answer accurately because they actually did it.

## Signals to watch (red flags during the call)

While the call is happening, listen for these noise signals and re-direct toward past behavior:

- **Compliments without past behavior**: "This is a great idea" / "I love this" / "I think this could be huge" → noise. The customer is being polite. Re-direct: "Tell me about your current workflow."
- **Future hypotheticals**: "I would totally use this" / "I'd be willing to pay" / "I'm planning to" / "I'm going to start" → noise. Future tense is wishful thinking. Re-direct: "What are you using today?" or "What did you pay last?"
- **Generic answers without specifics**: "I always do this" / "I usually have this problem" / "I often need this" → low signal. Probe for specifics: "Tell me about the last time" or "Walk me through this morning."
- **Projection onto others**: "Other people I know would love this" / "My team would use this" → noise. Probe: "Have you told them about [product]? Who specifically? What did you say?"
- **Validation-seeking** (the founder talking): when the conversation drifts to "what do you think of [feature idea]?" the founder is fishing. Cut it; return to "tell me about your workflow."

What IS signal:

- **Specific past behavior**: "Yesterday I" / "Last week I" / "This morning I" / "When I bought [tool] last year, I" → real data. Past behavior predicts future behavior.
- **Spontaneous referral**: "I told my colleague about you last month" / "I sent it to my brother" / "I mentioned you at [event]" → highest-grade signal. The customer voluntarily advocated. Probe: "What did you say? What was their reaction?"
- **Specific dollar memory**: "I paid $X for [tool] last month" / "We have a $Y budget for [category]" → reliable WTP data.
- **Workflow detail**: the customer walks through their actual day with timestamps and names of tools / colleagues / decisions → the goldmine. This is the prose source for hero copy.

## Who to call

Two cohorts of 10:

**Cohort A: paying customers.**
- Sample for variety: most-recent paid, longest-tenured paid, highest-revenue paid, lowest-engagement paid (about to churn).
- Goal: hear what made them pay, what they got that exceeded expectations, what almost made them not pay.

**Cohort B: free-tier users who haven't converted (after 30+ days of free use).**
- Sample for engagement: high-usage non-converted, mid-usage non-converted, low-usage non-converted.
- Goal: hear what's blocking the upgrade, what would change their mind, whether they're a wrong-segment user (and skipping is the right call).

## How to recruit

- Email each user a one-line invitation: "Hi, I'm [name], the founder. I'm spending 30 minutes with 20 customers this month to understand what's working and what isn't. Can we set up a half-hour call?"
- Offer a small thank-you (free month of paid, $20 Amazon card, T-shirt) when relevant.
- Don't recruit through a survey; survey responders self-select for survey-completers, not your target.
- Don't recruit only happy customers. The angry ones know more.

## What to do with the recordings

Within 7 days of each call cohort:

1. Transcribe (Otter, Whisper, paid service). Whisper API runs at <$0.01 per call.
2. Mark the verbatim quotes worth lifting. Look for: pain language, trigger language, comparison language, recommendation language.
3. Group quotes by theme. Themes that appear in 5+ calls are real. Themes appearing in 1-2 calls are anecdotes.
4. Rewrite the hero, subhead, FAQ, and one testimonial-quote section using only customer language. Ship the rewrite.
5. Run the new homepage for 30 days; measure conversion vs the previous version using the events from Cat 53.

## When to skip / defer customer discovery

- Brand has fewer than 30 days of customers (too thin a base).
- Brand is pre-launch (no customers to call yet).
- Founder explicitly opts out (some founders won't run discovery; recommend it once, then move on).
