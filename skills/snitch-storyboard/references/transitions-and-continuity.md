# Transitions and continuity for boards

Read when shots or scenes join. At each boundary, compare the last readable state of the
outgoing shot with the first readable state of the next. A cut can skip time or change
viewpoint, but the viewer still needs enough information to understand the change unless
disorientation is intentional.

## Boundary questions

1. **Action phase:** Was the action started, completed, or interrupted? A cut on action
   should pick up a compatible phase rather than replaying a completed action.
2. **Position and direction:** Which side of the action axis is each view on? What are
   travel direction and eyeline before and after? A visible turn, camera traverse,
   re-establishing view, or deliberate confusion can justify a crossing.
3. **Object and body state:** Who holds the prop, in which hand, where is it resting,
   what is open/closed, worn/damaged, visible/hidden? Distinguish invariant asset traits
   from mutable scene state.
4. **Knowledge and reveal:** What can each character and viewer know at the cut? Does an
   insert, reflection, reverse view, subtitle, or sound cue reveal a secret too early?
5. **Time, place, and light:** Does the next view imply elapsed time, a new location,
   or a source change? State the bridge if it matters to interpretation.

Record `shot/panel ID | entry state | visible change | exit state | next dependency`.
For a stationary hold, the next dependency may be the viewer's new understanding rather
than a changed prop. A state contradiction is a concrete problem; a departure from
conventional coverage is not automatically one.

## Transition intent

Name why the next view begins: matched gesture, eyeline, sound lead, revealed object,
reaction, new spatial information, or purposeful contrast. A graphic match can connect
shapes while still needing character/object continuity. A dissolve or overlap has one
elapsed timeline interval, not the sum of two full on-screen lengths. Do not assume a
reaction shot proves an unseen event happened; establish the cause where the story
requires it.

If a cut fails, propose the smallest repair: retime one pose, adjust gaze/hand state,
insert a motivated reorientation, keep the axis, or explicitly stage a crossing. Escalate
to the director when the repair changes a protected reveal, story action, or performance
intention. Escalate to production design when it requires a new wall/door/prop position;
to cinematography when it requires a camera or light redesign. Keep the board's decision
record explicit rather than silently rewriting another department's approved plan.
