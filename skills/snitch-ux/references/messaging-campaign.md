# The Messaging Campaign — Funnel Collateral That Carries the Message

How the brand message (see `brand-messaging.md`) gets deployed as a campaign: what collateral
exists at each funnel zone, how the offer is priced and framed, and how the sale is closed.
Derived from the same brand-messaging framework as that file. Two ordering rules govern
everything here: **messaging campaign before marketing campaign** (words first; channels, ads,
and funnels are built around the words — most teams do it backwards, starting with logos and
media buys), and **never do a deeper zone's job in a shallower zone** (explaining nuance in
the hero is "assuming you're on the fifth date when you never asked them out").

## The 5-3-3 shape

A complete campaign has three layers; miss one and the house is broken:

1. **Curiosity — 5 sound bites** (the front steps; see `brand-messaging.md`). Zero-load
   survival statements. "Super easy and fun to drive" is curiosity; "2.6L four-cylinder" is
   enlightenment. "Won't rust" — curiosity; "made of titanium" — enlightenment. Your
   mission/world-changing purpose is enlightenment too: nobody cares until after they care
   whether the product works.
2. **Enlightenment — at least 3 collateral pieces** (the porch): lead generators, webinars,
   demo videos, case studies, white papers, assessments, newsletters — each *repeating* the
   five sound bites, never introducing new ones. Price sets dwell time: a $10 product gets
   5 seconds of enlightenment (the label); a $500 product needs real porch collateral.
3. **Commitment — at least 3 incentives to buy now** (the front door). Most brands' door is
   "boarded up": there's porch content but no reason to walk through. Incentives are one of:
   a **limited-time bonus** (adds value; premium-safe), an **expiring discount** (works, but
   discounting devalues premium offers — prefer the bonus), or **exclusive access** (a class,
   club, or challenge — insider status, not a markdown; e.g. a "debt-free in 12 months"
   community contest). A **guarantee that converts your key claim into a promise** belongs
   here too: "if it doesn't pay for itself in two uses, return it."

**Stage the disclosure like dates.** There are things you say to get the first date and
things you must not say until the fourth. BELAY ($100M): the landing page carried one hook —
*fractional* ("full-time-assistant quality for 10 hours a week") — and the mechanics of how
remote staffing works waited for the sales call, where the objection naturally surfaces.
Heavy material isn't wrong, it's wrong *early*: someone who downloaded your lead generator
"agreed to carry more weight." Ask of every message: which date does this belong to?

**One script per audience, one problem per product.** Two-sided businesses (hosts/guests,
wholesale/retail) need entirely separate campaigns — blending two heroes produces mush.
Different product lines own different pains ("you're out of time" sells assistants; "I don't
want to log into QuickBooks" sells bookkeeping) — silo the message, the vernacular, and if
possible the sales conversation per line.

## Lead generators (the porch's workhorse)

- **One lead generator per revenue stream — not ten.** Point every video, post, and page at
  the same single asset; people download on the sixth-to-eighth exposure, and one funnel's
  follow-up emails can be dialed to perfection while ten can't.
- **Format ladder, easiest → hardest:** PDF checklist (1–2 hours to make, high perceived
  value) → educational guide with case studies → named niche newsletter → evergreen course →
  live webinar/event. Start with the checklist.
- **The PDF's internal arc** mirrors guide positioning: one problem (own that mental real
  estate) → title promising its resolution → empathy (the story of when you had this
  problem) → honest agitation (why it's serious) → a numbered ~3-step plan of genuinely
  useful advice → stakes → one concrete next action that puts skin in the game.
- **The lead generator is a sample of what buying is like** ("your lead generator should be
  an example of what you do"), and its title comes from a sound bite — including inversions
  of the change line ("5 signs the man you're dating is bad with money").
- **Qualification ladder:** cold downloader → automated weekly nurture; multi-week reader →
  personal email; anyone who paid even $49 → personal call within 48 hours.
- **The Michelin play (category-scale content):** publish the definitive *free* resource
  that increases the behavior your product depends on (Michelin, a tire company, published
  the guide that made people drive), over-invest in its integrity precisely because it
  doesn't have to make money, and guard the standards forever — dilution kills the asset.

## Entry offers & the product ladder

- **Unlock the front door.** Complicated service suites often have "the shop doors chained
  shut" — no simple first step a stranger can take. Productize one: an assessment, a
  workbook, a 5-page personal strategy built in one session — and aim all messaging at that
  single door, not the catalog.
- **An entry product converts a lead into a buyer**, and buyers behave differently
  (commitment/consistency: "I'm the kind of person who invests in this"). Rules: valuable on
  its own, impulse-priced, a taste of the real service, a stepping stone — never throwaway
  bait. Heuristic: charge ~10% of the value delivered.
- **Never jump $500 → $100K.** Insert rungs; ~10% of customers will pay 10× what they've
  already paid — but only if the next rung exists.
- **Charge for it or they won't respect it.** Free access to the core offer destroys
  engagement (and your case studies); discount instead of comping, and verbally anchor the
  recipient's investment.

## Pricing & premium framing

- **Anchor against the expensive alternative you replace, never the cheap competitor.** A
  $100 sharpener is positioned against $50–60/visit professional sharpening ("commercial
  grade in your kitchen"), not the $10 Amazon one — and gets a payback sound bite: "pays for
  itself in two uses."
- **Do the math for them.** Buyers won't do the arithmetic that proves your value, even with
  the numbers in hand. Pre-compute it: cost per use, cost per month against the familiar
  spend ("you'll spend $20/month on paper towels anyway"), the highlighted P&L lines they'll
  no longer pay. If your product saves a company $50K/year, you're not selling the product —
  you're selling $50K.
- **Stack the below-the-line value in words.** For any offer, write three lines — emotional
  (how they'll feel), social (the status/tribe payoff), philosophical (the bigger meaning) —
  and sell them alongside the physical thing. "You'll fall in love with exercise again +
  you'll be the leader of the pack + you're saving the environment" turns a $2,000 e-bike
  into a $5,000 one "and you didn't add anything except words." (They must be true.)
- **Three tiers, result-framed.** Confused buyers default to the cheapest option they
  understand. Cut menus to good/better/best, describe each tier by the outcome ("a showroom
  finish that keeps your car cleaner longer"), strip icons and ingredient jargon — a car-wash
  operator's top-tier sales rose ~12% in the first month.
- **Radical price transparency wins the trust race.** Publish the pricing page competitors
  refuse to: what drives price up/down, honest ranges, example projects with numbers,
  lifetime vs. initial cost, hidden costs, an "is it worth it?" discussion. (It's also the
  content answer engines love to cite.)

## Closing — the commitment zone's words

- **The decision-trigger close.** A fence-sitter is confused about exactly one thing: *"is
  this the right decision?"* They'll say "let me think about it," hoping the affirmation
  happens at home — it won't, because you don't go home with them. So the ask is always:
  **"If you're struggling with [problem], buying [product] is the right decision."** Tie the
  verdict to the named problem, never a bare "you should buy Y." Put it verbatim on the
  website, in proposals, in sales scripts.
- **Direct CTAs only — kill "Learn more."** Passive CTAs read as insecurity ("I don't
  believe my product solves your problem, but I have a mortgage"). "Buy now," "Schedule a
  call," "Reserve — €445 total." Cocktail-party test: if the ask would be weird said aloud
  to a person, it's weird on the website. Repeat the CTA: state it after the solution, paint
  the after-scene, state it again.
- **The fence-sitter email:** warm open → "I want to be really clear:" → restate and agitate
  the prospect's problem in their own words → "if I've heard you correctly, signing today is
  the right move because it solves this problem." (Stuck deals often close within 48 hours.)
- **Remove friction from the money path.** Count clicks to purchase — "you're going to get
  beat by somebody who doesn't make people click twice." Save the default order, find the
  recurring moment of need, and offer a one-tap yes at that moment (Domino's: "Football's
  about to start. Want your favorite pizza? Hit yes.").
- **Give an excuse to buy.** People buy when they have an excuse: launch-week pricing, a
  bundled bonus, a time-boxed program ("Suit Up September"). True deadlines only.

## Sales & content copy structures

- **Six-step sales email:** the prospect's voiced problem → empathy → your product as the
  solution to *that* problem → one differentiator ("what sets us apart…") → the
  decision-trigger affirmation → a concrete ask. Same skeleton for every product.
- **Subject lines: WIIFM or story loop — nothing else.** Either state what's in it for them
  ("40% off ends Friday" — never "It's that time of year again") or open a loop only the
  email closes ("The one thing every employee hates in a boss"). Cute and seasonal get
  deleted.
- **Video / keynote / post structure (the 7-beat):** problem (landed in the first ~6–8
  seconds) → agitate it (physical problem → its emotional cost) → solution → empathy →
  authority ("I solved this for…") → paint life after → CTA. Introduce yourself minutes in,
  never first — nobody cares who you are until they know what problem you solve. Tell your
  own version of the problem in the *past tense* ("I used to…") — present tense costs you the
  authority to solve it.
- **Testimonial video (the 21-second arc):** customer-hero in a hole by second 4 → raise the
  stakes ("it was killing me") → "it's not what you've tried" (disqualify their past
  failures — this reopens hope) → the plan over-delivers → the founder affirms the *customer*
  (never themselves) → pivot to the viewer with the after-scene → decision-trigger CTA,
  repeated. Keep written testimonials to one line.
- **Pitch decks: zero pages about your company.** Open with the cost of the problem in the
  buyer's money, escalate the problem for several slides before any solution, include a
  visual process map, and pre-answer every stakeholder objection (legal, compliance, "what
  do the OEMs say") inside the deck. One rebuilt deck "saved six months of selling cycle."
- **Demonstrate usage (show the scoop).** Buyers hold a silent objection: "once I know how
  to use it, I'll buy it." Show the literal steps — even when it feels stupid — where
  strangers can see it (website, video), not only in sales calls. Put the 3-step plan inside
  the ad itself ("Soak your Chia, spread the seeds, water") and spend words only on what the
  customer *can't* infer.
- **Preemptive objections / the anti-pitch.** Fold an answer to each unspoken objection into
  the pitch as blunt declaratives — including the downsides, stated confidently ("I'm more
  expensive than anybody else, and I'll take longer. But it's going to be done right.") —
  volunteered costs read as the absence of shortcuts and end comparison shopping.

## Repetition discipline

- **Marketing is an exercise in memorization.** Customers choose the offer they can
  *remember*, not the best one. If they can't recite your line, they can't repeat it to a
  spouse — word of mouth dies. Audit: reduce your site's central offer to a bumper sticker;
  if you can't, neither can they.
- **When you're bored of it, keep saying it.** The failure mode is the owner's fatigue, not
  the audience's: change the creative (image, format, angle), keep the sound bite verbatim.
  "Is it working? Then it's not time for a refresh." (MAGA won; "Keep America Great" lost;
  MAGA won again.)
- **You probably don't need a rebrand — you need more repetition.** Before any rebrand
  project, audit repetition first. And if you do rename, budget ~3 years of aggressive,
  internally-aligned repetition before the market re-finds you.
- **Mine incoming questions to pick what to repeat.** Whatever people keep asking, you
  haven't said enough. Do the message research before writing: read competitors' 3-star
  reviews ("wanted to like it, but didn't" — the honest complaint list), and mine reviews,
  forums, and support tickets for the customer's own emotional language about the problem;
  write in that language.

## When the message is bad news (price rises, broken promises, crises)

Messaging here mitigates damage; set that expectation. The playbook:

1. **Never make your problem the customer's problem** — reframe the change as value where
   real value exists ("Upgrade your experience," not "New policies, same heart").
2. **Name the downside yourself, once, plainly** — then stack the positives. If there's no
   value story, tell the truth about the economics instead ("our suppliers charge us more;
   to stay in business and pay our staff, prices rise") — honesty converts anger into
   loyalty; shareholder language ("return to the profitability our shareholders expect")
   converts loyalty into anger. Announce genuinely new features in a *separate* message so
   customers can't price-compare the apology.
3. **Show your work** to the commentator class — a short white paper on the business reality
   arms your defenders.
4. **In a trust collapse, repeat your critics' words louder than they do.** Domino's:
   "People told us our pizza tasted like cardboard — and they were right. We rebuilt it from
   the crust up." Nobody does this, so it seizes attention, and "if they admit it, they must
   be fixing it." Sequencing is strict: fix the product before the marketing.
5. **Escape the villain role with nuance.** Public villains are one-dimensional (deny
   everything or relish the harm), so be neither: "half of what they're saying is false —
   but here's the part that's true, and I take full responsibility for it," paired with a
   costly, visible corrective act. People judge the *contrast* between who you claimed to be
   and who you are — shrink the gap by claiming less and confessing sooner.

## Guardrails

Everything above amplifies whatever is underneath it — which is the argument for honesty,
not against technique. Deadlines and scarcity must be real; the guarantee will be exercised;
the payback math must survive a customer's spreadsheet; value-stacking lines must describe
real experiences; and a premium story ("10 years longer") obligates a premium offer behind
the door. See the reservoir-of-goodwill rules in the main skill.
