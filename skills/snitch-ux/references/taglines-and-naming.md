# Taglines, Names & Short-Format Copy

How to write the shortest surfaces — the tagline, the brand/product name, the billboard-sized
line (which is also the hero H1 and the ad headline). Derived from the same brand-messaging
framework as `brand-messaging.md`. The governing law for all of it: **the customer will not
spend calories decoding you.** A line that must be explained has already failed, because you
won't be there to explain it.

## Taglines

A tagline is a **plain-spoken offer tied to something the customer wants to survive or
thrive** (save money, make money, save time, look better, feel better, less anxiety, status,
belonging) — not a vibe, a value, or a poem. Aim for "it sounds like you wrote it on a
napkin": not clever, effective.

**The grading tests:**

- **Name-strip test.** Remove the company name and category: if the line could describe a
  church, a logistics firm, or a senior-living center, it fails. "Great prices, great people"
  fails; "Save big money at Menards" passes.
- **Stranger-guess test.** If a stranger heard only this line, how close do they get to
  guessing what you sell? "Belong anywhere" → Airbnb is the 733,000th guess. "Rent somebody's
  home for half the price of a hotel" → nearly the first.
- **Money test.** A tagline's only success metric is that it sells. "Kids love aquariums"
  stamped on the box raised sales 99% in the test market — parents no longer had to think.
- **The household-name exception.** Elusive, mood-only taglines ("Just do it," "Have a Coke
  and a smile") work *only* for brands whose product is already universally understood —
  maybe six to eight companies on Earth. Everyone else is still explaining what they sell.
  You can be cute or clever only when you are *also* clear; cleverness always costs clarity.

**Formulas that work:**

- **Say it plainly ("what are you trying to say?").** Keep asking the owner what they mean
  until they blurt the real claim — the blurt is the tagline. "Quality you can trust" →
  "We believe our houses should last 150 years." "Live life comfortably" → "The most
  comfortable chair ever made."
- **Anatomy: category + loop + objection kill.** First words name the category so nobody
  guesses wrong; the rest opens a story loop and preempts the objection the brain was about
  to raise. Magic Spoon: "Healthy cereal that tastes too good to be true" — *healthy cereal*
  (category), *tastes too good* (kills "healthy = cardboard"), *to be true* (loop: I have to
  try it).
- **Same-but-different.** "[Known thing] + one twist": the brain batches everything it knows
  about the familiar thing and you skip all category education. Cards Against Humanity: "A
  party game for horrible people." Slate truck: "the Mr. Potato Head of vehicles." Name
  exactly one difference — never two.
- **Command taglines.** Imperatives put the thought directly in the reader's head — no
  translation from claim to action. "Take your family to a baseball game" beats "Baseball:
  America's favorite family sport." "Hire a cowboy to build your fence" beats "Sitting on
  the fence? Call a Kowboy."
- **"You/your."** Given two candidates, pick the one that says *you*: "Your favorite plumber"
  beats "We are the greatest plumber ever."
- **The explainer tagline (complicated products).** State the product's function as a
  plain-verb category claim and attach it everywhere the name appears. ID.me: "How the
  internet proves it's you" — one line tells 150M passive users what the product is for.
- **Division of labor with the name.** If the name doesn't say what you sell, the tagline
  must say it literally (Nashville's *Local Honey*, which sells no honey: "We sell clothes.
  We do hair."). If the name is already clear (*Acme Plumbing*), the tagline is freed to
  carry the differentiator — ideally countering the category's known failure ("We're always
  on time").
- **Thread the controlling idea.** The controlling idea shouldn't sit beside the five sound
  bites — it should recur *inside* the problem, answer, and end-result lines. Men's
  Wearhouse: "You're going to like the way you look — I guarantee it" is simultaneously the
  answer, the change, and the end result (and the bolted-on guarantee de-risks the promise).

## Names

- **Bullseye test.** Say only the name/domain/handle to a stranger and ask what they think
  you do. If they guess right, you hit the bullseye; ~95% of names miss. "@FitFounder" —
  strangers correctly guess "fitness for entrepreneurs." medicaltraveloptions.com — nobody
  guesses "buy your prescriptions cheaper from overseas."
- **Name the outcome, not the mechanism.** Customers don't know they need your mechanism
  even when they do. The "Billie Jean King Eye Coach" (trains your eye to watch the ball)
  should be the "Sweet Spot Trainer" — same product, zero-load promise. Transition without
  repackaging: "Some people call it the ___."
- **Name from the customer's problem, not the founder's taste.** A branding exercise pulled
  "the well-placed smile" from a founder's favorite poem; everyone assumed *Well-Placed
  Smile* was a dentist. It was an event space.
- **The 1.5-second rule.** If the name doesn't say what it is (Magic Spoon reads as a
  kitchen gadget), the clarifying tagline must land within ~1.5 seconds of the name — before
  the brain guesses wrong, because un-thinking a wrong guess costs double.
- **Borrow the word the market already trusts (bridge words).** When customers resist a new
  technology, don't argue with their 30-year-old story — rename with a trusted word from the
  old world. Milwaukee's cordless line flopped until it was named **FUEL**: tradespeople went
  "it's the same as gas," and it became one of the most successful lines in trade history.
- **Track the market's live vocabulary.** Re-audit your category noun yearly: BELAY sold
  "executive assistants," renamed to "virtual assistants" when that's what buyers searched,
  then back to "executive assistants" when AI made "VA" mean a bot ("We are not bots").
  Clarity about what you are beats SEO inertia.
- **Package by industry.** The same product converts better when its name contains the
  buyer's industry ("The Dental Practice Marketing System," "The Chiropractic Growth
  Blueprint") — every buyer believes their industry is unique. If one version dominates,
  you've found your niche.
- **Name your features.** A christened feature unpacks a benefit; a described one doesn't.
  Bombas: "honeycomb arch-support system," "performance footbed," "blister tab" — scripted,
  benefit-loaded, repeatable. "Thicker on the bottom" implies nothing.
- **Print the built-in negative as the differentiator.** The thing you're embarrassed about
  is often the strongest honest claim if you word it first: a refrigerated health bar prints
  "Real food needs to be refrigerated" (indicting every shelf-stable bar); natural peanut
  butter's oil layer becomes "Simply stir and serve."
- **Provocative names for dull categories** are attention engines ("the Nut Grabber") — pair
  the playful surface with pain-focused sound bites underneath, and keep the provocation
  register matched to the category's stakes (fine for a party game; wrong for a dentist).

## Billboard rules (any ~3-second surface: OOH, hero H1, ad headline, social hook)

- **Make an offer that solves a problem; never run brand-awareness art.** Logo-plus-vibe
  works only for household names — the same double standard as elusive taglines. A 3M duct
  tape mood board is an A for 3M and an F for a startup; the startup must say "Does your
  duct tape leave residue? Ours doesn't."
- **~10 words maximum**, zero cognitive load. Graded winner (11 words): "Total Wine, Spirits,
  Beer and More — exit 74B to Brentwood Place."
- **Problem + who to call.** "Big truck wreck? Call this number."
- **Close the loop you open.** "Please give me a job" fails; "Please give me a marketing
  job" works — never leave the category open.
- **Put your name big.** A shrunken brand name reads as embarrassment.
- **Add a deadline or event when possible.** "Holiday lights at the zoo, now through
  December 31st" beats pure awareness.
- **If you must joke, use the Geico formula:** joke → punchline → brand association ("We
  can't fix your marriage, but we can clear your drain"). And remember the register rule:
  humor signals *discount*; premium brands don't joke about price.
- **Repetition compounds.** Buc-ee's wins with ~24 identical signs per exit, one short line
  each — consistency beats individual brilliance.

## Guardrails

Same bar as `brand-messaging.md`: the offer named must be one you deliver, the negative you
print must be true, and a guarantee is a promise you'll be held to. A clear line about a
false claim just helps people discover the lie faster.
