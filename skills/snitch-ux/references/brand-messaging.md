# Brand Messaging — Invite the Customer Into a Story

How to write the *brand-level* message — the homepage hero, tagline, value prop, onboarding
sequence, pitch — as opposed to the button-level microcopy in `copywriting.md`. Derived from
Donald Miller's StoryBrand framework (the "customer is the hero, you are the guide" model and
the five-sound-bites messaging pattern).

The core mistake this fixes: businesses *tell their story* ("we're passionate about
world-class solutions") instead of **inviting the customer into a story** in which the
customer uses the product to solve their problem. Nobody wants to hear your story; everyone
wants to be the hero of their own.

## The stance: customer is the hero, you are the guide

Every story is about **a hero in a hole** who has to get out. Your customer is that hero.
Your brand is not the hero — it's the **guide**: the character who leans over the hole, says
"I know how that feels," and throws down a rope. The rope is your product.

The load-bearing insight: **a rope is only valuable to someone in a hole.** Hand a stranger
a 20-foot rope and they mutter "thanks" and shove it in the garage. Throw the same rope to
someone stuck in a hole and it's priceless. Same product — the *problem* is what creates the
value. So:

- **Lead with the problem, always.** "I own a gym" earns a subject change. "I help men in
  their 50s add 10 years to their life" earns "tell me more." Every message that opens with
  the product, the company, or the founder's backstory is handing strangers ropes.
- **Order matters.** The personal why ("I watched my dad get old before his time…") is
  powerful *after* the problem is named, and inert before it. Problem → empathy → product →
  backstory, never backstory-first.

## The five sound bites (PEACE)

Five short, repeatable lines that together tell the whole story — and each of which also
works standing alone as an ad, a headline, or a cold-open. Acronym **PEACE**:

| # | Sound bite | Job | Worked example (You Need a Budget) |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | **Problem** | Hook — name the hole the hero is in | "Have you ever worried about money?" |
| E | **Empathy** | Position yourself as the guide who cares | "We know how you feel." |
| A | **Answer** | Throw the rope — place the product as the solution | "Download the YNAB app." |
| C | **Change** | The transformation — an *aspirational identity* | "…and get good with money." |
| E | **End result** | The climactic scene — what *life* looks like after | "…so you'll never have to worry about money again." |

Read in order they form one sentence: *"Have you ever worried about money? We know how you
feel. Download the YNAB app and get good with money — so you'll never have to worry about
money again."* That sentence is the brand's message. Everything else hangs off it.

### Rules for writing them

- **Zero mental calories.** No nuance, no inside language, no qualifiers. Bumper-sticker
  logic — a four-year-old could follow it. If a reader has to think, the step is too tall.
- **Don't disqualify the market with the problem line.** "Worried about money?" excludes
  anyone who isn't *right now*. "Have you **ever** worried about money?" captures ~100% of
  the market — and still hits 10× harder for the people currently in the hole.
- **The change line is a compliment test.** Write the transformation as an identity the
  customer would *love* to have said about them ("he's really good with money"). If it
  wouldn't land as a compliment, it's not aspirational enough.
- **End result = what life looks like**, not what the product does. The vacation, the calm,
  the never-worrying-again — the closing scene of the movie, not the feature list.
- **Each line works alone *and* in sequence.** Any single sound bite should be able to run
  as a standalone ad; the five together must read as one coherent story.
- **Adaptable per audience, anchored per brand.** One canonical set for the brand; adapted
  variants per product or audience ("I help parents keep up with their kids" for the couple,
  "best serve in the game" for the tennis kid) — as long as each still opens with a problem.
- **Curse of knowledge is the enemy.** The team is too close to the product to write these
  without effort — expect hours of wrestling, not minutes. The hard part isn't writing the
  five lines; it's **leaving everything else out**.
- **Close the loop you opened (the act-one gun rule).** The problem line opens a story loop;
  the end-result line must close *that exact loop*, not a neighboring one. "Have you ever
  worried about money?" → "…so you'll never have to worry about money again." Mismatches
  sound wrong even when every sentence is fine: "Restlessness and anxiety can ruin your
  life — we created a sleep mask" opens an anxiety loop and closes a sleep loop; rewrite the
  problem to match the product. Same rule per asset: only present features that close the
  loop the headline opened.
- **One problem only — commas are not your friend.** A story is about one thing; value-
  stacking dilutes. If several problem angles are true (speed, cost, back pain), pick the
  one with the strongest felt resonance and save the others for *separate* campaigns.
- **The hole must be specific.** "A hero that lacks fulfillment" sells no movie tickets;
  "Liam Neeson's daughter has been kidnapped" does. Replace the category problem
  ("misbehaving dogs") with one lived scene ("your dog barks when someone knocks and it
  drives you crazy"). Hyper-specific niching *sells more*, not less: a generic "solar
  security alarm" loses to "Raccoons keep raiding your trash cans" — and you can run several
  parallel hyper-niche campaigns (raccoons / dogs / break-ins) for the same product.
- **Claim a survival stake.** The reader's brain asks one question — "how does this help me
  survive/thrive?" — and gives you seconds to answer. Every problem line should map to one
  of: make money, save money, save time, ease anxiety, sleep/feel better, gain status, or
  belong. If it maps to none, it's not a problem line yet.

### Completing the script — the full seven slots

PEACE is the compressed set. The complete BrandScript has a few more slots; every one should
exist as a written sound bite (and appear somewhere on the website):

- **The want** — one short line stating what the customer wants. One thing, never ten.
- **The problem at three levels** — external (the physical problem), internal (how it makes
  them feel), and philosophical (*why it's unjust*: "it's unfair that great businesses lose
  to bad messaging"). The internal and philosophical levels are where most brands leave
  money: people buy solutions to internal problems. The philosophical line doubles as a
  mission: "We exist so that [person] no longer has to experience [pain]."
- **The guide needs two lines, not one** — empathy ("we know how it feels") *plus*
  competency/authority (proof you can get them out: numbers, track record, awards). "A giant
  loving heart toward your customer, and big old muscles to solve their problem."
- **A three-step plan.** Interested customers stall because buying feels foggy and risky; a
  named plan of exactly three baby steps replaces the unpredictable future with step 1-2-3
  ("Buy a Breeze card → board → enjoy smooth travel"). Step 1 must be trivially easy. Bake
  the likely objections into the steps.
- **The call to action, two flavors** — the direct ask ("Buy now," "Schedule a call" — never
  "Learn more") and the **decision-trigger affirmation**: "If you're struggling with X,
  buying Y is the right decision." The fence-sitter's only real question is "is this the
  right decision?" — answer it verbatim. (Full closing scripts: `messaging-campaign.md`.)
- **Stakes, both ends** — a success line (life after) *and* a failure line (the no-good
  thing that happens if they don't buy). Something must be won or lost. Failure framing is
  "a low blow, but a very effective blow" — use it gently, and only if true.
- **Identity transformation** — the before/after pair: "from frustrated commuter to
  empowered city explorer."

### The one-liner — the message at handshake scale

The memorized, formulaic answer to "what do you do?": **problem → product → result**, in one
to three short sentences. "You know how most families don't eat together anymore, and when
they do, they don't eat healthy? I'm an at-home chef. I come to your house and cook, so your
family can connect around the dinner table instead of cooking and cleaning up." Two identical
chefs at a party — the one who says this gets all the business; "I'm an at-home chef" gets a
subject change.

- The **product line must be concrete and procedural** enough to kill silent objections:
  "I'm a therapist. I meet with you twice and I tell you whether or not you should get
  married" (A grade), not "I help you figure out whether to get married" (C-minus — is there
  a test? do we meet separately?).
- The **result must be specific, never elusive**: "so you know you married the right
  person," never "so you can be fulfilled."
- **Stress test:** after you say it, people should say "tell me more." If they change the
  subject, it isn't dialed in — even people *without* the problem should want your card for
  the aunt who has it.
- **Founder-story variant** for "tell me about your company": "I got tired of seeing people
  struggle with X. So I created Y, and I sell it to solve that problem."
- **Deploy it**: back of the business card, website subtitle under the header, email
  signatures, bios, the whole team drilled to say it. "Your one-liner is a hook in a pond —
  the more hooks in the pond, the more fish you catch."

### The villain

Every brand story benefits from a villain — with strict rules:

- **It's the customer's villain, never yours.** Villainize the thing driving the *customer*
  crazy (Magic Spoon villainized sugar), never your own grievance against incumbents or
  gatekeepers (Tidal launched on "we artists deserve more money" — a pitch for the 0.001%
  who are artists — and was ignored until customers heard the customer-side story).
- **The villain is the root cause, not the problem itself.** Fill-in test: "What ___ is
  doing to our customers isn't right." (StoryBrand's villain is *confusion*.)
- **Two concrete words beat a paragraph.** "Cardboard feel" (Bombas, on tube socks),
  "cardboard taste." Exaggeration is fine if it's instantly recognized.
- **Ask what your product removes.** The George Foreman grill sold $5M/yr as the "Fajita
  Express" (speed angle); repositioned on what it *removes* — "Knock out the fat" — the
  identical product did $400M. If the message isn't landing, systematically test other
  problem-angles before touching the product.
- **Never name a competitor.** Naming them gives them attention and triggers comparison
  shopping. If you must invoke the alternative, do it as an innocent question the customer
  answers themselves ("Who's going to make the bed while you're away?"). But do **say the
  contrast out loud** when the industry has burned people: "Most programs certify you and
  never talk to you again. We're not like them —" + the structural reason you can't repeat
  the harm. And if every competitor *does* something but none *says* it, the first to say it
  owns it ("Women make the first move" — Bumble's whole $8B differentiator was said, not
  invented).
- **Darkness budget: ~80–95% problem, 5–20% candle.** You cannot skip negativity — the brain
  only notices problems — and counterintuitively, the messenger who paints the darkness
  honestly and keeps the plan short is remembered as the *optimist* ("the darker the room,
  the more the candle stands out"; Churchill; Dave Ramsey). If everything is positive,
  you're one of a thousand candles in a lit room. End in hope: fear gets attention, hope
  gets action.

## The controlling idea — be known for one thing

People leave a page (or a conversation) carrying **one** thing, not ten bowling balls of
information. The **controlling idea** is that one thing — the moral of the brand's story,
usually crystallizing out of the Change or End-result sound bite ("Get good with money"
became YNAB's tagline).

- "Known for something specific" beats "known for the category." Five tennis coaches in
  town; the one *known for building the best serve in the game* gets the business. "I work
  with female athletes" is a category; "I make female athletes fast" is a controlling idea.
- The controlling idea governs everything downstream: every ad, lead magnet, keynote, and
  onboarding email must obey it. Off-script messaging confuses; confusion kills conversion.
- A specific controlling idea also **prices the offer**: a "$90/mo gym membership" versus a
  "$1,000/mo *10 Years Longer* cohort" — same building, different story, 11× price. (The
  program must then actually deliver that story — see Guardrails.)

## Audits — five checks that find the leaks

- **Weigh the copy in pounds; the header must weigh zero.** Assign every phrase a cognitive
  weight: decoder-ring poetry ("where style meets purpose" +20), irrelevant history ("since
  2017" +15), your mission (+15), invented categories ("the modern human" +25),
  self-congratulation (+10). A 100-lb paragraph gets ignored. Evidence this is causal, not
  taste: a barista-course ad rewritten to zero load went from 18 to 125 clicks in the same
  48 hours; YNAB's header went from "Change your relationship with money" (~20 lbs) to
  "Never worry about money again" (0). Heavy material isn't wrong, it's wrong *early* —
  weight is earned as commitment deepens.
- **The Sharpie test.** Print the homepage and circle every place it names the customer's
  problem with the product positioned as the solution. Target: **10+ circles**. A supplement
  company went $9M → $18M in two years by rewriting product descriptions to open with the
  pain ("gut rot and how it makes you tired") instead of the mechanism ("how we dehydrate
  broccoli into a pill"). If you own a problem, you own the market.
- **The 5-second test.** Show a stranger the site for five seconds, close the laptop, and
  ask: (1) What problem do they solve? (2) What will my life look like after? (3) How do I
  buy it? All three or you're losing money — almost always because secondary messaging
  (about you) is sitting where primary messaging (about them) belongs.
- **"What do you mean by that?"** When copy sounds sophisticated, ask the author what they
  meant — the spoken answer is always the clear version; ship the answer. "Integrated,
  scalable solutions to optimize operational efficiency" → "we help small businesses save 10
  hours a week by automating their billing." Say the clear part, not the clever part.
- **"I'll think about it" is a confusion diagnostic.** Customers never say "your cognitive
  load is too high"; they say "I'll think about it" (= "I'm going home to figure out what
  you just said") or silently go to a competitor. When you hear it, the fix is clarity, not
  more persuasion.

## Your story has a place — just never first

- **Primary vs. secondary messaging.** Primary (lead with, always): why you care about the
  customer's problem, why you're competent to solve it, how you solve it, and the results.
  Secondary (never lead with): your history, values, vision, goals, passions, and why you
  beat competitors. Secondary material becomes appropriate after ~5–10 interactions or after
  purchase. "You beat the competition by sticking to your primary messages while they stick
  to their secondary ones." Hero brands fail; guide brands win ("I'm with her" vs. "she's
  with us"; WeWork, Theranos, CNN+ all told the founder's story, not the customer's).
- **The About page is the guide's backstory** — only the part of your history that explains
  why you care about the customer's problem and how you became competent to solve it. Four
  beats: the hole you (or your first customer) were in → the tool you built to get out →
  why getting others out became the mission → the transformation customers now live. Same
  pool company, two true stories: "our kid got sick from an over-chlorinated pool and we
  decided pool care should be done better" grows the business; "I wanted money and liked
  working in shorts" doesn't. Attach every credential to the customer ("tell me you went to
  school there because you loved solving this problem").
- **The long version is entertainment for the trusted** — an 8-beat company story
  (hole → calling → refinement → dark night → miracle → breakthrough → the *customer's*
  better world → one-line moral), alternating negative/positive beats like a screenplay.
  Timing rule: it's told "over drinks after" the pitch landed — the About page video, the
  3rd–4th onboarding email — never to open. Someone in a hole begging for help does not want
  to hear where you were born.

## Where the message lives: front steps → porch → door

Map the funnel to a house. The job of each zone is different — most messaging fails by doing
the wrong job in the wrong zone:

- **Front steps = the five sound bites.** Their job is *curiosity*, not truth. You are not
  educating, explaining nuance, or proving anything — you're making a stranger stop and say
  "that sounds like something I need; tell me more." Most businesses have no front steps at
  all: their first message is an 8-foot climb ("we are a leading provider of…").
- **Front porch = enlightenment and trust.** Webinar, demo, lead magnet, white paper,
  keynote, YouTube deep-dive. *Here* is where explanation and proof belong — for people the
  steps already brought up.
- **Front door = the paywall.** The purchase decision, made by someone who now trusts the
  guide.
- **Inside the house = nuance.** The sophisticated concepts the team loves ("it's really
  about intentional spending, not restriction") belong *after* purchase — that's how paying
  customers become evangelists. Putting nuance on the front steps just makes them steep.

## Deploy it everywhere (a message is a campaign)

The five sound bites are worthless on a napkin. They pay when shipped with campaign-level
discipline — same words, everywhere, for years (Chick-fil-A drilled "my pleasure" for three
years; that's a messaging campaign):

- **Website:** hero H1 = problem or change line; tagline next to the logo = controlling
  idea; welcome blurb = the full five-line sentence. (This is exactly the "convey the big
  picture" slot in `site-navigation.md` — the tagline there *is* the controlling idea.)
- **Onboarding:** five consecutive emails, one sound bite each. In-product UI copy that
  echoes the sound bites — audit the actual interface strings and swap generic words for the
  message ("your budget" → "get good with money").
- **Lead generators & content:** title every asset from a sound bite, including inversions
  of the change line ("5 signs the man you're dating is *bad* with money"). Every clip,
  short, or post covers exactly one of the five — never a sixth idea.
- **Spoken:** the answer to "what do you do?" is the problem line, not the job title. The
  keynote is the five sound bites stretched to 80 minutes; the elevator pitch is the same
  five at 8 seconds. **Same formula, any length.**
- **Ritual sound bites:** a fixed line at a fixed moment ("Remember: we're doing this to
  live 10 years longer" at the end of every session) re-justifies the price at every
  touchpoint.
- **The stickiness test:** if a listener can repeat your five sound bites back after one
  exposure, they can *sell for you*. If they can't, the lines aren't done.

The full campaign machinery — the 5-3-3 structure, lead generators, entry offers, pricing
framing, closing scripts, and the bad-news playbook — lives in `messaging-campaign.md`.

  about money?").

## Guardrails

Same honesty bar as the rest of this skill. The problem must be one the product genuinely
solves; the empathy must be real (manufactured "we feel your pain" from a brand that
demonstrably doesn't reads as manipulation and empties the goodwill reservoir); the change
and end result must be transformations real customers actually experience — an aspirational
identity you can't deliver is just a lie with good structure. And a premium story ("10 years
longer") obligates a premium offer behind the door — charging story prices for a commodity
product spends trust you won't get back.
