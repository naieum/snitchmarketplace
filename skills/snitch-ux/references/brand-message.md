# Brand Message — the Message on the Surface

The *brand-level* message as it appears on a screen — the homepage hero, the tagline beside
the logo, the value prop, the welcome blurb, the first onboarding line — as opposed to the
button-level microcopy in `copywriting.md`. Derived from a widely-taught brand-messaging
framework that ports story structure into marketing copy, refined across many thousands of
business rewrites.

The seam with snitch-cmo is drafts vs. judgment: snitch-cmo drafts the hero, one-liner, and
tagline options that fall out of positioning; this file judges the one on the page against the
visitor's decision path and rewrites the microcopy around it. For a blank page — brand story,
company/product/feature naming, sales collateral, or the option set itself — call the Skill tool
with "snitch-cmo".

> Gate first: `ethics-gate.md`. A clear line about a false claim only helps people discover
> the lie faster.

The core mistake this fixes: a page *tells the company's story* ("we're passionate about
world-class solutions") instead of **inviting the customer into a story** in which the
customer uses the product to solve their problem.

## The stance: customer is the hero, you are the guide

Every story is about a hero in a hole. The visitor is that hero; the brand is the **guide**
who throws down a rope. The rope is the product — and **a rope is only valuable to someone in
a hole.** The *problem* is what creates the value.

- **Lead with the problem, always.** "I own a gym" earns a subject change. "I help men in
  their 50s add 10 years to their life" earns "tell me more." Any hero that opens with the
  product, the company, or the founder's backstory is handing strangers ropes.
- **Order matters.** Problem → empathy → product → backstory, never backstory-first. The
  personal why is powerful *after* the problem is named and inert before it.

## The five sound bites (PEACE)

Five short, repeatable lines that together tell the whole story, each of which also works
standing alone as a headline:

| # | Sound bite | Job |
|---|---|---|
| P | **Problem** | Hook — name the hole the hero is in |
| E | **Empathy** | Position the brand as the guide who cares |
| A | **Answer** | Throw the rope — place the product as the solution |
| C | **Change** | The transformation — an *aspirational identity* |
| E | **End result** | What *life* looks like after |

Read in order they form one sentence — a budgeting app's runs *"Have you ever worried about
money? We know how you feel. Download the budgeting app and get good at money — so you'll
never have to worry about money again."* That sentence is the message; everything on the page
hangs off it.

Rules for grading them:

- **Zero mental calories.** No nuance, no inside language, no qualifiers. If a reader has to
  think, the step is too tall.
- **Don't disqualify the market with the problem line.** "Worried about money?" excludes
  anyone who isn't right now; "Have you **ever** worried about money?" captures nearly the
  whole market and still hits hardest for the people currently in the hole.
- **The change line is a compliment test.** Write the transformation as an identity the
  customer would love to have said about them. If it wouldn't land as a compliment, it isn't
  aspirational enough.
- **End result = what life looks like**, not what the product does.
- **Close the loop you opened.** The end-result line must close the exact loop the problem
  line opened. "Restlessness and anxiety can ruin your life — we created a sleep mask" opens
  an anxiety loop and closes a sleep loop. Same rule per section: only present features that
  close the loop the headline opened.
- **One problem only — commas are not your friend.** If several angles are true (speed, cost,
  back pain), pick the one with the strongest felt resonance, and make the hole specific:
  replace the category problem ("misbehaving dogs") with one lived scene ("your dog barks when
  someone knocks and it drives you crazy").
- **Claim a survival stake.** Every problem line should map to one of: make money, save money,
  save time, ease anxiety, sleep/feel better, gain status, or belong. If it maps to none, it
  is not a problem line yet.

## The one-liner — the message at handshake scale

**Problem → product → result**, in one to three short sentences: "You know how most families
don't eat together anymore, and when they do, they don't eat healthy? I'm an at-home chef. I
come to your house and cook, so your family can connect around the dinner table instead of
cooking and cleaning up."

- The **product line must be concrete and procedural** enough to kill silent objections: "I
  meet with you twice and I tell you whether or not you should get married," not "I help you
  figure out whether to get married." The **result must be specific, never elusive**: "so you
  know you married the right person," never "so you can be fulfilled."
- **Stress test:** people should say "tell me more." A subject change means it isn't dialed
  in. On the surface it is the subtitle under the header and the About-page opener.

## The controlling idea — be known for one thing

Visitors leave carrying **one** thing, not ten. The controlling idea is that one thing —
usually crystallizing out of the Change or End-result line. "I work with female athletes" is a
category; "I make female athletes fast" is a controlling idea, and the second one gets the
business.

The tagline beside the logo **is** the controlling idea (that is the "convey the big picture"
slot in `navigation.md`). Everything downstream on the surface — section headers, onboarding
copy, in-product strings — obeys it; off-script messaging confuses, and confusion kills
conversion.

## Where it lives on the page

- **Hero H1** = the problem line or the change line. The hero's job is **curiosity**, not
  education: make a stranger say "that sounds like something I need." Explanation, proof, and
  nuance belong further in — after the demo, after the paywall, after purchase. Nuance in the
  hero just makes the first step an 8-foot climb.
  **Scope this before you write it up.** The rule is about the *message* the hero carries, not
  about which element is largest. A product name as the H1 with the problem or change line
  reading directly beneath it — the conventional brand-name-plus-tagline hero — already
  delivers the message in the block a visitor reads as one unit; that is a Pass, not a
  hierarchy finding. This becomes a finding only when the hero block as a whole leaves the
  visitor without the problem or the change: a name with a category label under it, a
  backstory, or a value prop the visitor has to hunt for further down the page. Never open a
  finding on the H1's *size* or on which line "should" be promoted when the message is
  already there.
- **Tagline** next to the logo = the controlling idea.
- **Welcome blurb** = the full five-line sentence, compressed.
- **In-product strings** echo the sound bites: swap generic UI words for the message ("your
  budget" → "get good at money").
- **Primary vs. secondary messaging.** Primary (lead with, always): why you care about the
  customer's problem, why you're competent, how you solve it, the results. Secondary (never
  lead with): history, values, vision, goals, passions, why you beat competitors. Secondary
  material earns its place deeper in the surface, not above the fold.

## Taglines

A tagline is a **plain-spoken offer tied to something the customer wants to survive or
thrive** — not a vibe, a value, or a poem. "It sounds like you wrote it on a napkin" is the
target: not clever, effective.

**The grading tests:**

- **Name-strip test.** Remove the company name and category: if the line could describe a
  church, a logistics firm, or a senior-living center, it fails.
- **Stranger-guess test.** If a stranger heard only this line, how close do they get to what
  you sell? "Belong anywhere" leaves a home-rental marketplace hundreds of thousands of
  guesses away; "rent somebody's home for half the price of a hotel" lands nearly first.
- **The household-name exception.** Mood-only taglines work only for brands whose product is
  already universally understood — maybe six to eight companies on Earth. Be cute only when
  you are *also* clear; cleverness always costs clarity. A tagline's only success metric is
  that it sells.

**Formulas that work:**

- **Say it plainly.** Keep asking what the line means until someone blurts the real claim —
  the blurt is the tagline. "Quality you can trust" → "our houses should last 150 years."
- **Anatomy: category + loop + objection kill.** A low-sugar cereal: "healthy cereal that
  tastes too good to be true" — *healthy cereal* names the category, *tastes too good* kills
  the "healthy = cardboard" objection, *to be true* opens a loop.
- **Same-but-different.** "[Known thing] + one twist" skips all category education. Name
  exactly one difference, never two.
- **Command taglines.** Imperatives put the thought straight in the reader's head: "take your
  family to a baseball game" beats "baseball: America's favorite family sport." Given two
  otherwise equal candidates, pick the one that says *you*, not *we*.
- **The explainer tagline** for a complicated product states its function as a plain-verb
  category claim, attached everywhere the name appears.
- **Division of labor with the name.** If the brand name doesn't say what you sell, the
  tagline must say it literally. If the name is already clear, the tagline is freed to carry
  the differentiator — ideally countering the category's known failure ("we're always on
  time"). Judge the pair as it renders in the header, within about 1.5 seconds of each other:
  un-thinking a wrong guess costs double.

Naming pages and sections is `navigation.md`'s street-sign rule, not this file's. Company,
product and feature naming is off-surface — call the Skill tool with "snitch-cmo".

## Audits — the checks that find the leaks

- **Weigh the copy in pounds; the header must weigh zero.** Assign every phrase a cognitive
  weight: decoder-ring poetry ("where style meets purpose" +20), irrelevant history ("since
  2017" +15), your mission (+15), invented categories ("the modern human" +25),
  self-congratulation (+10). A 100-lb paragraph gets ignored. Heavy material isn't wrong, it's
  wrong *early* — weight is earned as commitment deepens.
- **The Sharpie test.** Print the page and circle every place it names the customer's problem
  with the product positioned as the solution. Target: **10+ circles.**
- **The 5-second test.** Show a stranger the page for five seconds, close the laptop, and ask:
  (1) What problem do they solve? (2) What will my life look like after? (3) How do I buy it?
  All three, or the page is losing money — almost always because secondary messaging is
  sitting where primary messaging belongs.
- **"What do you mean by that?"** When copy sounds sophisticated, ask the author what they
  meant — the spoken answer is the clear version. Ship the answer, not the clever part.
- **"I'll think about it" is a confusion diagnostic.** Nobody says "your cognitive load is too
  high." When you hear the stall, the fix is clarity, not more persuasion.

## How this composes with the rest of the skill

Write the message here → polish every line with `copywriting.md` → build the sentences with
`writing-system.md` and score them with the linter → place it with `navigation.md` (tagline,
welcome blurb, entry points). The problem line is the skill's "one question" model at brand
scale: it replaces the hard question ("want to buy budgeting software?") with an easy one
("have you ever worried about money?").

## Guardrails

Same honesty bar as the rest of this skill. The problem must be one the product genuinely
solves; the empathy must be real (manufactured "we feel your pain" reads as manipulation and
empties the goodwill reservoir); the change and end result must be transformations real
customers actually experience — an aspirational identity you can't deliver is a lie with good
structure. The offer a tagline names must be one you deliver, and a guarantee is a promise
you'll be held to.
