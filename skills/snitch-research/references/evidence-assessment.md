# Assess evidence for the claim being made

Read when the investigation contains competing accounts, samples, quantitative comparisons,
or consequential conclusions. Apply only the parts needed by the questions derived from the task.

## Work backward from the claim

Write the claim narrowly enough to test. Determine what observation would support it, what
would contradict it, and which conditions must hold for that observation to apply here.
Choose sources by their ability to supply that evidence. A primary source can have incentives
and weak methods; a secondary source can supply useful independent analysis. Neither label
settles credibility.

Inspect the underlying material where accessible. Capture the relevant passage, table,
method, or observation with a precise locator in working notes; quote sparingly in the report.
For documents, use page/section/table; for code, file and line; for web material, URL and
section or selector; for media, timestamp and the modality actually inspected. A transcript
does not establish visuals, and sampled frames do not establish uninterrupted viewing.
Do not imply an experiment or direct observation occurred when only a report was read.

Separate the source's assertion from the claim we want it to establish. If evidence supports
only a narrower version, narrow the conclusion rather than attaching a hedge to an unsupported
generalization. Distinguish supplied constraints from supplied factual assertions: honor the
former, assess the latter.

## Trace independence and disagreement

Follow citations, identical wording, shared datasets, and publication chronology back to the
underlying origin. Treat syndication, paraphrases, and repeated quotations as one evidence
family. Several analyses of the same data may provide independent interpretations but do not
create independent observations. Record that dependency where it affects confidence.

For a material disagreement, compare the actual propositions before declaring a contradiction:
definitions, dates, populations, conditions, and methods may differ. If they address the same
claim, explain which evidence is more applicable and why. If the conflict cannot be resolved,
retain it and lower the strength of the affected conclusion, not unrelated conclusions.

Search for the strongest plausible competing account rather than selecting an easily dismissed
objection. Consider whether selection effects, confounding, missing cases, or changed conditions
could explain an apparent pattern. State what evidence could distinguish those explanations.
Do not invent a controversy when strong evidence consistently supports one answer.

## Samples and accounts

Record where accounts came from, the selection method, period, inclusion boundaries, and
accessible sample size when known. Group recurring themes without erasing differing contexts
or positive counterexamples. Check for duplicates and shared origins before counting reports.

A firsthand account can strongly support what that person reports experiencing while offering
weak support for how often it happens generally. Separate the event, its proposed cause, and
its prevalence. Do not turn a count of selected accounts into a population rate. Lack of reports
does not prove lack of an event, especially when reporting or access is restricted.

Describe patterns as observed in the inspected sample. A rare but consequential event can
deserve further investigation even without a frequency estimate; neither severity nor volume
alone establishes its relevance to this task. Translate evidence into a proposed response
only after assessing whether the circumstances transfer. Make the needed validation explicit.

## Numbers and comparisons

Keep the measured quantity attached to its definition, unit, time period, population, scope,
and method. Align those before ranking or combining values. If alignment is impossible,
explain why the values are not directly comparable and avoid a misleading total or ranking.

Keep reported measurements, estimates, projections, and your calculations distinct. Show
inputs and assumptions for derived quantities, preserve meaningful ranges, and avoid precision
the inputs cannot support. A proxy does not become the target measure because the target is
unavailable. Missing values remain missing, never zero. An aggregate cannot establish a
component's value without supporting data.

Comparisons need applicable alternatives and baselines, not just the most visible examples.
Inspect what made cases succeed or fail when those claims can be supported, but do not infer
causation from the outcome alone. State limits on transfer to the user's circumstances.

## Prioritize the next question

Use the entrypoint's scales. For each important unresolved question, identify what answering
it could change and what evidence is missing. Prefer the next action that can resolve
consequential uncertainty over accumulating more sources for an already supported point.

Deepen a branch when a relevant claim rests on weak evidence, strong sources conflict, a
dependency could invalidate the conclusion, or a new consequence changes the task's stakes.
Do not deepen solely because a topic has abundant material. If a branch loses relevance,
record why and stop it. If progress is blocked, name the specific evidence or access needed;
do not repeat ineffective searches to simulate thoroughness.

An explicit resource limit may leave important questions open. Return the useful evidence and
identify which conclusions remain provisional. Do not mark the investigation resolved because
the allotted time expired.

## Check synthesis for bias

Before delivering, ask:

- Would the same evidence produce materially the same assessment if the user preferred the
  opposite answer? Would I demand the same proof from a favored and an unfavored source?
- Have I hidden an unwelcome conclusion behind praise, softened it without evidence, or used
  a stronger headline than the body supports?
- Have I confused preserving the user's decision authority with accepting its factual premises?
- Have I included material contrary evidence and assessed it rather than merely listing it?
- Have I assigned equal weight to unequal evidence, or manufactured a negative conclusion to
  appear skeptical?
- Is each recommendation supported by the evidence and its applicability, with tradeoffs and
  uncertainty intact? What observation would cause me to revise it?

Correct the assessment, not just its tone. Present a conditional conclusion where conditions
matter, an unfavorable conclusion where supported, and an honest unknown where evidence ends.
