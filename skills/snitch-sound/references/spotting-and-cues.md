# Spotting and cue relationships

Use this when a scene needs more than one sound layer or when a cue crosses a cut.

## What each layer does

| Layer | Question | Common handoff need |
|---|---|---|
| Dialogue or vocalization | Which words, breaths, or vocal efforts carry information? | Supplied line and performance intent; recording or replacement status |
| Ambience | What stable place surrounds the listener, and when does it change? | Location or designed bed, room tone, transition, perspective |
| Event effect | Which action or off-screen event needs an onset and spatial source? | Prop/action sync, recording/design need, decay and tail |
| Music | What emotional or structural change requires it? | Entry/exit, competing dialogue, source/rights status |
| Deliberate absence | What should the audience notice because a layer leaves? | Which layer remains, how long, what returns it |

Treat a scene's acoustic geography as continuity: source location, distance, obstruction,
interior/exterior boundary, and listener position. A sound may lead the image, bridge a cut,
or arrive after a visual event; label which relationship is intended. Do not make every
on-screen movement audible merely because a matching effect could be found.

For a paper plan, time in seconds relative to the scene and mark estimates. For inspected
picture or audio, cite its file/version and actual timestamps. Source timecodes and sequence
timecodes are different; a trim or speed change must not silently transfer one to the other.
Use separate cue IDs for separate events, but one sustained ambience may span many shots.
Report overlaps as simultaneous layers, not extra elapsed screen time.
