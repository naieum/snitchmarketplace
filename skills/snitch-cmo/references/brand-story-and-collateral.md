# Brand story and campaign collateral

The off-page half of the message: what the brand is against, how its story is told where a
page cannot tell it, what to call things, and the collateral that carries the message from
first contact to the sale. Foundation mode reads this when `brand-voice.md` and
`channel-plan.md` need more than a voice — when the user is building the assets, not just the
posts. Drafting mode reads the section matching the asset it is drafting.

**Boundaries.** The words *on* the page — hero, one-liner, tagline, the five sound bites — are
*drafted* by this skill: they fall out of the positioning, and `positioning-method.md` § 3
writes the hero options. What belongs to snitch-ux is the judgment once they sit on a page:
the tests that grade them (cognitive weight, five-second, Sharpie) against the visitor's
decision path. When a draft here leans on one of those words and it needs grading in place,
call the Skill tool with "snitch-ux". Restructuring one persuasive page section by
section belongs to snitch-focusedcopy — call the Skill tool with "snitch-focusedcopy". Grading
what is already live is snitch-marketing's job. This file is for the assets the brand *makes*,
which is why it lives with the channel playbooks.

Everything here amplifies whatever is underneath it, which is the argument for honesty rather
than against technique. Deadlines and scarcity are real or absent. A guarantee will be
exercised. Payback math survives the buyer's own spreadsheet. Every value line describes an
experience real customers have. The evidence gate and the ethics gate in SKILL.md apply to
every asset below without exception.

## The villain

A brand story lands harder when something is on the other side of it. The rules are strict:

- **It is the customer's villain, never the brand's.** Villainize the thing driving the
  *customer* crazy — sugar, downtime, the invoice nobody can read. A grievance against
  incumbents or gatekeepers is the founder's story, and it speaks to the sliver of the
  audience who share it.
- **The villain is the root cause, not the symptom.** Fill in the blank: "what ___ is doing to
  our customers isn't right."
- **Two concrete words beat a paragraph.** A texture, a taste, a sound. Exaggeration is fine
  when it is instantly recognized as such.
- **Ask what the product removes**, not only what it adds. Products repositioned around what
  they take away — the fat, the wait, the second login — have outperformed the same product
  sold on what it does. When a message is not landing, test other problem angles before
  touching the product.
- **Never name a competitor.** Naming one hands them attention and starts comparison shopping.
  Invoke the alternative as a question the buyer answers themselves. The exception is a
  category that has burned people: say the contrast out loud, then give the structural reason
  this brand cannot repeat the harm.
- **Darkness budget: mostly problem, a little candle.** The brain notices problems, so the
  message that names the difficulty honestly and keeps the plan short is the one remembered as
  optimistic. All-positive copy is one candle in a lit room. End in hope: fear gets attention,
  hope gets action.

`positioning.md` records the villain in one line. Every draft that uses it inherits that line
rather than inventing a new adversary per post.

## The About page and the long company story

The About page is not the brand's autobiography. It is the part of the history that explains
why this team cares about the buyer's problem and how they became competent to solve it. Four
beats:

1. The hole the founder — or the first customer — was in.
2. The tool built to get out of it.
3. Why getting other people out became the work.
4. The transformation customers now live in.

Attach every credential to the customer: the school, the certification, and the years all
exist in the copy to prove the problem gets solved, not to decorate a bio.

The long version — an eight-beat company story alternating hard and good beats: hole →
calling → refinement → the worst moment → the break → the breakthrough → *the customer's*
better world → a one-line moral — is entertainment for people who already trust the brand.
Its slots are the About-page video, the third or fourth onboarding email, the podcast
interview. It never opens a relationship. Someone in a hole asking for help does not want to
hear where the founder was born.

The same discipline governs order everywhere: why you care about their problem, that you can
solve it, how, and the results — those lead. History, values, vision, and why you beat the
competition follow, after several interactions or after purchase.

## Naming: company, product, feature

- **The bullseye test.** Say only the name — no tagline, no context — and ask a stranger what
  the company does. Most names miss. A handle that reads as "fitness for founders" hits; a
  descriptive-sounding domain that describes the wrong thing misses badly.
- **Name the outcome, not the mechanism.** Buyers do not know they need the mechanism even
  when they do. A trainer named for the thing it trains beats one named for how it works.
- **Name from the customer's problem, not the founder's taste.** Names pulled from a favorite
  poem read as a different industry entirely.
- **The 1.5-second rule.** If the name does not say what the thing is, the clarifying line has
  to land almost immediately — before the brain guesses wrong, because un-thinking a wrong
  guess costs twice.
- **Borrow the word the market already trusts.** When buyers resist a new technology, do not
  argue with the story they have carried for thirty years; rename with a word from the world
  they trust. Product lines have gone from flop to standard on the rename alone.
- **Re-audit the category noun yearly.** The word buyers search shifts, and sometimes shifts
  back when a term gets colonized by something else. Clarity about what the thing is beats
  inertia in the keyword data.
- **Package by industry when the same product serves several.** Buyers believe their industry
  is unique, and a name containing it converts better. If one version dominates, the wedge
  scoring has been confirmed for free.
- **Name the features.** A christened feature unpacks a benefit and gets repeated; a described
  one does neither. "Thicker on the bottom" implies nothing.
- **Print the built-in negative as the differentiator.** The property the team is faintly
  embarrassed by is often the strongest honest claim, if worded first: the thing that must be
  refrigerated indicts everything that does not.
- **Provocative names work in dull categories** — paired with pain-focused lines underneath,
  and only where the register fits the stakes.

## Short-format surfaces: OOH, ad headlines, social hooks

Any surface that gets about three seconds. The hero H1 obeys the same physics, but that one is
graded on-page by snitch-ux.

- **Make an offer that solves a problem; do not run awareness art.** Logo-plus-mood works for
  brands everyone already understands. Everyone else has to say the thing.
- **Around ten words, zero decoding.** If a line has to be explained, it has failed — nobody
  will be standing there to explain it.
- **Problem plus who to call** is a complete billboard.
- **Close the loop you open.** Never leave the category open to a guess.
- **Put the name big.** A shrunken name reads as embarrassment.
- **Add a real deadline or event** where one exists; it beats pure awareness.
- **If joking: joke, punchline, brand association** — and remember humor signals discount, so
  premium brands do not joke about price.
- **Repetition compounds.** A dozen identical short lines beat a dozen clever different ones.

## Repetition discipline (the message is a campaign, not a document)

A message is worthless on a napkin. It pays when it is shipped everywhere, in the same words,
for years.

- **Onboarding**: consecutive emails, one message beat each; product strings echoing the same
  words rather than generic ones.
- **Assets and content**: every title comes from one of the message lines, including
  inversions of the transformation line. Each clip, post, or short covers exactly one line,
  never a sixth idea.
- **Spoken**: the answer to "what do you do?" is the problem, not the job title. The keynote is
  the same lines stretched to an hour; the elevator pitch is the same lines in eight seconds.
  Same formula, any length.
- **Ritual lines**: a fixed sentence at a fixed moment re-justifies the price at every
  touchpoint.
- **The stickiness test**: if a listener can repeat the lines back after one exposure, they can
  sell for the brand. If not, the lines are not finished.
- **Marketing is an exercise in memorization.** Buyers choose the offer they can remember, not
  the best one. If the central offer will not compress to a bumper sticker, word of mouth has
  nothing to carry.
- **When the team is bored of it, keep saying it.** Fatigue is the owner's problem, not the
  audience's: change the creative, keep the line verbatim. The documented failures are lines
  retired while they were still working.
- **Most brands do not need a rebrand, they need more repetition.** Audit repetition before
  any renaming project, and budget years of aggressive repetition if the rename happens.
- **Mine incoming questions for what to repeat.** Whatever people keep asking, the brand has
  not said enough. Before writing, read the category's three-star reviews — the honest
  complaint list — and mine forums and support tickets for the buyer's own words about the
  problem. Write in that language.

## The campaign shape: curiosity, enlightenment, commitment

A complete campaign has three layers, and missing one breaks it:

1. **Curiosity** — the short lines that make a stranger stop. Zero mental load. Their job is
   not truth or proof; it is "that sounds like something I need." These live on the page and
   are snitch-ux's surface; this file assumes they exist.
2. **Enlightenment** — at least three pieces of collateral: lead generators, demos, case
   studies, assessments, guides, the newsletter. Each *repeats* the curiosity lines; none
   introduces a new one. Price sets how much of this is needed — a cheap impulse product needs
   a label, a considered purchase needs the whole shelf.
3. **Commitment** — at least three honest reasons to act now. A limited-time bonus (adds
   value, safe for premium offers), an expiring discount (works, but discounting erodes a
   premium story — prefer the bonus), exclusive access (a cohort, a class, a club — insider
   status rather than a markdown), or a guarantee that converts the key claim into a promise
   ("if it doesn't pay for itself in two uses, send it back"). Most brands have the middle
   layer and no reason to walk through the door.

**Stage the disclosure.** Heavy material is not wrong, it is wrong *early*. Someone who
downloaded the guide agreed to carry more weight than the stranger reading the headline. Ask of
every message: how far into the relationship does this belong?

**One script per audience, one problem per product.** Two-sided businesses need two campaigns;
blending both heroes produces mush. Different product lines own different pains — silo the
message, the vocabulary, and where possible the sales conversation per line.

## Lead generators

- **One per revenue stream, not ten.** Point every post, video, and page at the same asset.
  People act on the sixth to eighth exposure to the *same* thing, and one follow-up sequence
  can be tuned to perfection where ten cannot.
- **Format ladder, easiest first:** a checklist (a couple of hours to make, high perceived
  value) → a guide with real examples → a named niche newsletter → an evergreen course → a
  live event. Start at the checklist.
- **The asset's internal arc:** one problem → a title promising its resolution → empathy (the
  time the team had this problem) → honest agitation → a numbered three-step plan of genuinely
  useful advice → the stakes → one concrete next action.
- **It is a sample of what buying is like.** The asset should be an example of the work, not a
  teaser for it. Its title comes from one of the message lines.
- **Qualification ladder:** a cold downloader gets the automated nurture; a multi-week reader
  gets a personal email; anyone who has paid anything at all gets a personal reply quickly.
- **The category-scale play:** publish the definitive *free* resource that increases the
  behavior the product depends on — the classic case is a company that sold something people
  used while driving publishing the guide that made people drive. Over-invest in its integrity
  precisely because it does not have to earn; dilution kills the asset.

## Entry offers and the product ladder

- **Unlock the front door.** Complicated service menus leave a stranger with no simple first
  step. Productize one: an assessment, a workbook, a single-session strategy — and aim the
  messaging at that door, not the catalog.
- **An entry product turns a lead into a buyer**, and buyers behave differently afterward:
  they have become the kind of person who invests in this. Rules: valuable alone,
  impulse-priced, a real taste of the work, a stepping stone — never throwaway bait. A working
  heuristic is charging around a tenth of the value delivered.
- **Never jump from a small price to an enormous one.** Insert rungs. A slice of customers will
  pay ten times what they last paid — but only if that rung exists.
- **Charge something, or it will not be respected.** Free access to the core offer destroys
  engagement and the case studies with it. Discount rather than comp, and name the value of
  what was given.

## Pricing and premium framing — the words, not the number

The number itself is decided elsewhere (`positioning-method.md` for the strategy read;
snitch-blueprint for a greenfield price). This is how the offer is *framed*.

- **Anchor against the expensive alternative being replaced, not the cheap competitor.** A
  premium tool is positioned against the recurring cost of doing it the old way, and earns a
  payback line: "pays for itself in two uses."
- **Do the arithmetic for the buyer.** They will not do it, even holding the numbers. Publish
  the cost per use, the cost per month against a spend they already accept, the line item they
  stop paying. A product that saves a company a large annual number is not selling a product;
  it is selling that number.
- **Stack the below-the-line value in words.** Write three true lines — emotional (how they
  will feel), social (the standing it gives them), philosophical (the larger meaning) — and
  sell them alongside the physical thing. Words move the perceived value of an identical
  product enormously, which is exactly why they must be true.
- **Three tiers, described by result.** Confused buyers default to the cheapest option they
  understand. Cut the menu to good/better/best and describe each by the outcome it produces,
  not by its ingredients.
- **Radical price transparency wins the trust race.** Publish the page competitors refuse to:
  what drives the price up and down, honest ranges, worked examples with numbers, lifetime
  versus initial cost, the hidden costs, and an honest "is it worth it?" discussion. It also
  happens to be the page answer engines quote.

## Closing copy

- **The decision-trigger close.** A fence-sitter is confused about exactly one thing: *is this
  the right decision?* They say "let me think about it" hoping the affirmation arrives at home,
  and it never does. So the ask is always tied to the named problem: "if you're struggling with
  [problem], buying [product] is the right decision." Put it verbatim on the site, in
  proposals, in scripts.
- **Direct calls to action only.** "Learn more" reads as insecurity. "Buy now", "Book the
  call", "Reserve — total shown". The test: if the ask would be strange said out loud to a
  person, it is strange on the page. Repeat it — after the solution, after the after-scene,
  at the end.
- **The fence-sitter email:** warm open → "I want to be really clear:" → restate and sharpen
  their problem in their own words → "if I've heard you right, signing today is the right move,
  because it solves this." Stalled deals often move within days.
- **Remove friction from the money path.** Count the clicks to purchase. Save the default
  order. Find the recurring moment of need and offer a one-tap yes at exactly that moment.
- **Give an honest excuse to buy** — launch pricing, a bundled bonus, a time-boxed program.
  Real deadlines only; a manufactured one is a dark pattern, and the ethics gate blocks it.

## Sales and long-form structures

- **Six-step sales email:** their problem in their words → empathy → the product as the
  solution to *that* problem → one differentiator → the decision-trigger affirmation → a
  concrete ask. The same skeleton works for every product.
- **Subject lines: what's in it for them, or an open loop the email closes.** Nothing else.
  Seasonal and cute get deleted.
- **Video, keynote, or long post — seven beats:** the problem in the first few seconds →
  agitate it (the physical problem, then its emotional cost) → the solution → empathy →
  authority (this has been solved before, here) → life after → the ask. Introduce yourself
  minutes in, never first, and tell your own version of the problem in the past tense — present
  tense costs the authority to solve it.
- **Testimonial video, roughly twenty seconds:** the customer is in the hole within four
  seconds → the stakes rise → "it's not what you've tried" (disqualifying past failures
  reopens hope) → the plan over-delivers → the founder affirms *the customer*, never themselves
  → pivot to the viewer with the after-scene → the ask, repeated. Written testimonials stay one
  line. The creator has to have used the product: scripting experience someone has not had is a
  fabricated review and the ethics gate blocks it.
- **Pitch decks: zero pages about the company.** Open with the cost of the problem in the
  buyer's own money, escalate it for several slides before any solution appears, include a
  visual map of the process, and pre-answer every stakeholder's objection — legal, compliance,
  procurement — inside the deck.
- **Demonstrate usage.** Buyers hold a silent objection: once I know how to use it, I'll buy
  it. Show the literal steps, even when it feels too obvious, where strangers can see it —
  not only on sales calls. Put the three-step plan inside the ad itself and spend words only on
  what the buyer cannot infer.
- **Preemptive objections.** Fold an answer to each unspoken objection into the pitch as blunt
  declaratives, downsides included and stated confidently. Volunteered costs read as the
  absence of shortcuts and end comparison shopping.

## When the message is bad news

Price rises, broken promises, outages, and public criticism. Messaging mitigates damage here;
say so before drafting, because nothing below makes bad news good.

1. **Never make the company's problem the customer's problem.** Reframe as value where real
   value exists; do not dress a policy change as a gift.
2. **Name the downside once, plainly, then stack what is true and positive.** With no value
   story, tell the truth about the economics — costs rose, staff have to be paid, so prices
   rise. Honesty converts anger into loyalty; investor language converts loyalty into anger.
   Announce genuinely new features in a *separate* message so nobody price-compares the
   apology.
3. **Show the work** to the people who will argue on the brand's behalf. A short, factual
   explanation arms the defenders.
4. **In a trust collapse, repeat the critics' words louder than they do.** The turnarounds
   that worked led with the accusation verbatim and the admission that it was fair. Almost
   nobody does this, which is why it seizes attention — and admitting it reads as proof of the
   fix. The sequence is strict: fix the product, then say anything.
5. **Escape the villain role with nuance.** Public villains are one-dimensional. Be neither
   the total denial nor the shrug: "half of this is false — and here is the part that is true,
   which I take responsibility for," paired with a costly, visible corrective act. People
   judge the gap between who a brand claimed to be and who it is; shrink the gap by claiming
   less and confessing sooner.
